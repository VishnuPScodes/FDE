## What you're about to do

You'll write resume bullets and a project README that a busy hiring manager can understand in thirty seconds. These are the last pieces before you apply.

## The idea

Hiring managers skim. They look for what you did and what changed. A strong bullet has three parts: a verb, a thing, and a number. "Cut loan file checking from 45 to 20 minutes for three loan officers by building a missing page checker."

Use strong verbs: built, cut, reduced, shipped, led, measured. Avoid "worked on" and "responsible for". Put the number inside the sentence. Keep each bullet to one or two lines.

A README is the front door of your project. It should answer five questions fast. What it does: one or two sentences, no jargon. Who it is for: the real or imagined customer. Results: numbers, even if from a pilot or test data, labelled honestly. How to run: three to five commands someone can copy. What I would do next: shows you can think beyond version one.

Be honest. If results come from a practice scenario or test data, say so. Hiring managers respect honesty and quickly spot exaggeration. Do not include real customer data, keys or names of real people.

Finally, link to a short demo video or screenshots. One minute of video beats five paragraphs.

## A small example

> Built a WhatsApp order reader for a fictional Indore distributor, cutting data entry from 3 hours to 2.2 hours a day on test data, with a human review step.

Notice "fictional" and "test data". The claim is honest and still strong.

## Common mistakes

Listing technology names only. "Python, FastAPI, Postgres" says nothing about the problem you solved.

A README that starts with installation. Start with why.

Numbers with no source. Say where they came from.

## On the job

Customers also read your READMEs. A clear one is a sign you will write clear handover notes.
