## What you're about to do

You'll write short design documents for work you plan to do for a customer. A design doc is how you get agreement before you build. It saves weeks of rework.

## The idea

A design doc is not a report. It is a short argument: here is the problem, here is what I will build, here is what I will not build, and here is why.

Keep it to one or two pages. Use these headings. Context says what is going on and why it matters. Goals lists what success looks like, with numbers. Non-goals lists what you are deliberately not doing. Proposed design explains the solution in plain steps. Alternatives considered shows two or three other options and why you did not pick them. Open questions lists what you still need answered.

Non-goals are the most useful heading. They stop scope creep. If the customer later asks for something on that list, you can point to the page they already agreed to.

Alternatives matter too. They show the customer you did not jump at the first idea. "We considered buying a ready tool. It costs ₹6 lakh a year and does not read Malayalam forms."

Add a short rollout and rollback plan for anything that touches live data. Rollback answers: if this goes wrong on Monday morning, how do we go back?

## A small example

> Non-goals: We will not change the core banking software. We will not handle loan approval decisions. We will not support scanned Tamil forms in this phase.

Three lines, and a future argument is already settled.

## Common mistakes

Writing a long story of everything you know. Readers stop at page two.

Leaving out alternatives, so the customer wonders why you chose this path.

Hiding open questions. An honest list of unknowns builds trust.

## On the job

Send the doc before the meeting, not after. People who read first ask better questions.
