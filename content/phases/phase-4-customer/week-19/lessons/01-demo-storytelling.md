## What you're about to do

You'll plan demos for customers who are not technical. You'll write a script, plan for things going wrong, and practise finding out what the audience cares about before you show anything.

## The idea

A demo is not a tour of features. It is a short story about one person's bad day getting better.

Use five beats. Hook names the pain in the audience's own words. Before shows how the work looks today, with the real time or cost. Live moment shows the tool solving one real case, ideally with the customer's own data. After shows the same task now, with the new time. Ask says what you want next: a pilot, a name, a date.

Keep it under ten minutes. Show one case, not twenty. People remember the moment the tool did something that used to take an hour.

Things will go wrong. The Wi-Fi drops, the file is too large, the tool gives an odd answer. Plan for it. Know your likely failure, have a line ready to say, keep a recorded fallback, and move on calmly. A calm recovery builds more trust than a perfect run.

Before the demo, ask who will be in the room and what each person wants to see. A branch manager wants fewer complaints. A CFO wants cost. A clerk wants less typing.

## A small example

> Hook: "Every month-end your team copies shipping bill numbers by hand."
> Before: "Fifty hours a month, two people."
> Live moment: matches 180 invoices in two minutes on your own file.
> Ask: "Can we run it on March's data next week?"

## Common mistakes

Showing every feature. The audience remembers none of them.

Using fake data. It does not feel real. Use the customer's anonymised file.

Apologising too much when something breaks. Stay steady and move to the fallback.

## On the job

The demo is often where a pilot is approved or quietly dropped. Prepare as if it is the whole project.
